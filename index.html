Most cost-efficient

Why cheapest: Corporate credit spread means less capital needed up front to fund the same liability. Lower funding cost than Treasuries. Liability: Stays on balance sheet. No defeasance.

1. Classical single-period immunization
Single liability
Match asset duration to a single liability at a fixed future date. Continuous rebalancing required. Assumes parallel shifts only.
Conditions: Asset PV ≥ Liability PV Asset duration = Liability duration Asset convexity ≥ Liability convexity
2. Multiple liability immunization (Fong & Vasicek)
Multiple liabilities
Same three conditions PLUS the asset duration distribution must bracket the liability duration distribution — some assets shorter AND some longer than the liability range. Matching portfolio duration alone is insufficient.
3. Cash flow matching
Twist-immune
Directly pair asset cash flows to each liability date. No rate assumption. No rebalancing. Immune to all shifts — parallel and non-parallel. Eligible for defeasance if Treasuries used.
4. Contingent immunization
Active + safety net
Pursue active management while portfolio value exceeds the trigger. Switch to full immunization immediately when value hits the trigger.
Trigger = PV(liability) at current rates Cushion = Portfolio value − Trigger Active management stops when Cushion = 0
Risk: a sudden large rate move can erode the cushion faster than the manager can react, forcing immunization at an unfavorable level.

Why twists break duration matching — and the one thing that reduces the damage.

The root cause
Duration assumes one uniform rate change across all maturities. Under a twist, short-end and long-end rates move differently. Asset cash flows (spread across maturities) and liability cash flows (concentrated at one date) experience different effective rate changes — creating a funding gap even with a perfect duration match.
Bullet vs barbell — why it matters for twist risk
Bullet portfolioBarbell portfolio
Cash flow distributionConcentrated near liability dateSpread at short and long ends
ConvexityLower (closer to liability)Higher (more dispersed)
Twist exposureLower — all CFs experience similar rate changeHigher — short/long CFs diverge under twist
Parallel shift protectionSame — both duration-matched
Exam rule: to reduce non-parallel shift risk → minimize convexity (bullet structure). NOT maximize cash flow yield. NOT minimize duration gap.
Statement 1 (measurement errors on known liability) → Correct: duration/convexity parameter errors still apply even when the liability amount and date are certain.

Statement 2 (cash flow matching mitigates non-parallel shift risk) → Correct: structural match = immune to all yield curve shapes.
Step 1 — Screen on convexity (hard rule, no exceptions)
Any portfolio with convexity below the liability convexity is immediately eliminated. Non-negotiable — no BPV proximity can save it.
Asset convexity < Liability convexity → ELIMINATED
Step 2 — Smallest BPV gap among survivors
Smallest gap = fewest futures contracts needed = least basis risk and transaction cost.
Nf = (BPV_liability − BPV_portfolio) / BPV_futures BPV_futures = BPV_CTD / conversion factor Negative Nf = short futures · BPV gap = |portfolio BPV − liability BPV|
Step 3 — Don't maximize convexity
Excess convexity = more dispersed cash flows = more twist exposure. Prefer convexity just above the liability level, not maximized.

AM constructed response structure
1. State overall verdict: effective for parallel shifts, fails for twists 2. Cite the specific numbers from the table — yield gap AND MV difference for each scenario type 3. Explain the mechanism: parallel → both sides respond equally; twist → assets and liabilities sit at different maturities and experience different rate changes 4. Conclude: residual structural exposure to non-parallel shifts that duration matching cannot eliminate
Yield Curve Strategies · Interactive Study Map
Receive-fixed swap — two return components
Duration extension tool
What it does: Synthetically increases portfolio duration. You receive the fixed rate and pay MRR (floating). No bond purchase needed — pure rate exposure. Component 1 — Carry: Fixed rate − MRR = daily accrual. Accrues daily, settled periodically (quarterly/semi-annually). Can be positive or negative. Component 2 — Mark-to-market: As rates move, the swap's present value changes. Not a cash flow — only realized if you exit. Rates fall → swap gains value. Rates rise → swap loses value.
Carry settlement
Periodic (quarterly)
MTM settlement
On exit only
Duration effect
Increases ✓
Carry: can be positive OR negative
ConditionCarryWhen it happens
Fixed rate > MRRPositive ✓Normal upward-sloping curve — you earn the spread daily
MRR > Fixed rateNegative ✗Inverted curve or aggressive hike cycle — you pay out the difference
Worst case for receive-fixed: rates rise sharply → carry turns negative AND MTM loses value simultaneously. You lose on both legs.
Pay-fixed swap — the mirror image
You pay the fixed rate and receive MRR. Synthetically reduces or negatives portfolio duration. Profits when rates rise (MTM gain) and when MRR > fixed rate (positive carry in inverted curve). A bearish manager uses pay-fixed swaps (or shorts futures) to push total portfolio duration negative.
Negative portfolio duration — how to achieve it
Bonds always have positive duration — you cannot get negative duration from physical bonds alone. You need derivatives sized large enough to more than offset the positive duration of the bond holdings.
Tools: pay-fixed swaps · short bond futures · buy bond puts Size the notional so: |Derivative duration| > Portfolio bond duration Net duration = Bond duration − Derivative duration offset → negative
If duration = −2: rates rise 100bps → portfolio gains ~2%. Behaves like a short bond position.

Identification is on the government curve. Implementation uses swaps and futures.

How to identify: watch 2s10s spread on the government curve
2s10s = 10-year yield − 2-year yield. Spread widening = steepening. Spread narrowing = flattening. Identify bull vs bear: decompose what each end actually did. If 2yr fell 30bps and 10yr unchanged → bull steepening. If 10yr rose 30bps and 2yr unchanged → bear steepening.

What drives the 2-year yield
Almost entirely monetary policy expectations over the next 24 months. Central bank hikes / hawkish signals → 2yr rises immediately Central bank cuts / dovish pivot → 2yr falls immediately Hot CPI or strong payrolls → more hikes priced in → 2yr rises The 2yr barely cares about 2030 — it's anchored to what the CB does right now
What drives the 10-year yield — two components
1. Expected future short rates: what do investors think policy rates will average over 10 years? Connects to monetary policy at a much longer horizon. 2. Term premium: extra compensation for locking up money for 10 years. Captures inflation uncertainty, fiscal risk, government bond supply, global demand for safe assets.

What key rate duration (KRD) actually measures
Sensitivity of portfolio value to a rate change at one specific maturity point on the yield curve, holding all other maturities constant. Unlike overall duration which assumes parallel shifts.
KRD at 10yr = +6 means: 10yr rate falls 1% → portfolio gains ~6% 10yr rate rises 1% → portfolio loses ~6% (other maturities unchanged)

Cook example — downward parallel shift 50bps
Cook expects rates to fall. She wants to benefit. Long receiver swaption = right to receive fixed. If rates fall, she exercises and receives an above-market fixed rate. The swaption gains value as rates fall. Correct position ✓ Short receiver swaption = she sold the right to someone else. If rates fall, the counterparty exercises against her — she loses. Wrong direction.
The most common exam trap: confusing which swaption benefits from which rate direction. Anchor it to the underlying swap logic — receiver swaption = you want to receive fixed = you want rates to fall so your fixed rate looks attractive. Payer swaption = you want to pay fixed = you want rates to rise so paying the lower locked-in fixed rate is valuable.
Steepening trade with swaptions — summary
Steepening = long rates rise, short rates fall Long payer swaption at 30yr → right to pay fixed at 30yr → profits when 30yr rates rise ✓ Long call on 2yr bond → right to buy 2yr bond → profits when 2yr bond price rises = 2yr rates fall ✓ Combined = pure steepening bet with limited downside (premium paid)
The full expected return formula
E(R) = Rolldown return + (−Duration × Δyield) + (0.5 × Convexity × Δyield²) + ΔFX (if unhedged foreign bond)
Never drop the convexity term — even if the yield move seems small. The exam tests whether you include it. With 50bps move it's tiny (0.02%) but it's the difference between answer choices.
Component 1 — Rolldown return
The price appreciation from a bond "rolling down" an upward-sloping stable yield curve as time passes. A 2-year bond becomes a 1-year bond after one year — and since 1-year yields are lower, its price rises.
Rolldown = (Expected price in 1yr − Price today) / Price today Example: (98.75 − 97.00) / 97.00 = 1.804%
The expected price in 1yr assumes an unchanged yield curve — it is the price the bond would have if it simply aged one year with no rate moves. It is given in the exhibit, not calculated from scratch.
Component 2 — Duration + Convexity price effect
Price effect = (−Duration × Δyield) + (0.5 × Convexity × Δyield²) Example (bullet, −50bps shift): = (−4.50 × −0.0050) + (0.5 × 17.0 × 0.0050²) = +2.250% + 0.021% = 2.271%
Use the expected duration and convexity in 1 year (from exhibit), not today's values. Δyield is negative for rate falls. Always convert bps to decimals: 50bps = 0.0050.
Component 3 — Currency effect
If the foreign currency appreciates vs your home currency → add positive FX return If the foreign currency depreciates vs your home currency → subtract FX loss Cook example: USD depreciates against EUR by 0.75% → USD-denominated returns lose 0.75% when converted to EUR → −0.75%
Watch the direction carefully. "USD depreciates against EUR" = your USD bonds are worth less in EUR terms = negative FX contribution. Don't flip the sign.

Rolldown strategy — why it works
Buy further out on an upward-sloping curve. As time passes the bond rolls toward a lower-yield, higher-price point. You earn rolldown return on top of yield — as long as the curve stays stable and upward-sloping. This is why the 2-year zero-coupon (bought at 96.1169, expected to be worth 99.0090 in a year) earns more than the 1-year zero-coupon in the Thai bond example.
BPV / DV01 — same thing, different names
Dollar change in portfolio value for a 1bp (0.01%) move in rates.
BPV = Modified duration × Market value × 0.0001 Example: Duration 5 · MV $10M → BPV = 5 × $10M × 0.0001 = $5,000 per bp
Why it matters: Duration is a percentage — you can't subtract durations across portfolios of different sizes. BPV puts everything in the same unit (dollars per bp) so arithmetic works. Hedging: Nf = (BPV_target − BPV_portfolio) / BPV_futures Immunization: match asset BPV to liability BPV so a 1bp move hits both equally
Negative convexity — callable bonds
At high yields: call is out of the money, callable bond behaves like option-free bond — normal positive convexity. At low yields: call is deep in the money. Issuer's right to call becomes very valuable. Market knows bond may be called → price stops rising at normal pace → price-yield curve bends the wrong way → negative convexity. The asymmetry: less upside when rates fall (call caps price) but full downside when rates rise (call not exercised). You keep all the risk, lose some of the reward.
Why callable bonds must offer a higher yield: compensation for this asymmetric payoff. You are short the call option → you must be paid for it.
Forward rate bias
What theory says (UIP): high-yield currency should depreciate by exactly the yield differential — no free lunch. What actually happens: high-yield currencies depreciate by less than predicted, or not at all. The yield advantage is not fully offset. Implication: go unhedged into high-yielding currencies to earn excess returns. Amplify by borrowing in low-yield currencies (carry trade).
Carry trade = borrow in low-yield currency (JPY at 0.5%) · invest in high-yield currency (BRL at 10%) · pocket the spread. Works because forward rate bias means the low-yield currency doesn't appreciate enough to wipe out the carry.
Stable yield curve — what generates excess return
When rates don't move, you cannot earn excess return from predicting rate direction. The only way to beat the benchmark is to earn more carry.
StrategyWorks under stable curve?Why
Increase duration above benchmarkYesLonger bonds earn more yield + rolldown without rate rise penalty
Add leverageYesBorrow at short rates, invest in longer bonds, pocket the spread
Reduce duration below benchmarkNo — least likelyShorter bonds earn less yield, no rate protection needed, guaranteed underperformance vs benchmark
Reducing duration only makes sense if you expect rates to RISE — then shorter portfolio loses less than benchmark. Under a stable curve it just sacrifices yield for nothing.

Steepening trade with swaptions — summary
Steepening = long rates rise, short rates fall Long payer swaption at 30yr → right to pay fixed at 30yr → profits when 30yr rates rise ✓ Long call on 2yr bond → right to buy 2yr bond → profits when 2yr bond price rises = 2yr rates fall ✓ Combined = pure steepening bet with limited downside (premium paid)
The full expected return formula
E(R) = Rolldown return + (−Duration × Δyield) + (0.5 × Convexity × Δyield²) + ΔFX (if unhedged foreign bond)
Never drop the convexity term — even if the yield move seems small. The exam tests whether you include it. With 50bps move it's tiny (0.02%) but it's the difference between answer choices.
Component 1 — Rolldown return
The price appreciation from a bond "rolling down" an upward-sloping stable yield curve as time passes. A 2-year bond becomes a 1-year bond after one year — and since 1-year yields are lower, its price rises.
Rolldown = (Expected price in 1yr − Price today) / Price today Example: (98.75 − 97.00) / 97.00 = 1.804%
The expected price in 1yr assumes an unchanged yield curve — it is the price the bond would have if it simply aged one year with no rate moves. It is given in the exhibit, not calculated from scratch.
Component 2 — Duration + Convexity price effect
Price effect = (−Duration × Δyield) + (0.5 × Convexity × Δyield²) Example (bullet, −50bps shift): = (−4.50 × −0.0050) + (0.5 × 17.0 × 0.0050²) = +2.250% + 0.021% = 2.271%
Use the expected duration and convexity in 1 year (from exhibit), not today's values. Δyield is negative for rate falls. Always convert bps to decimals: 50bps = 0.0050.
Component 3 — Currency effect
If the foreign currency appreciates vs your home currency → add positive FX return If the foreign currency depreciates vs your home currency → subtract FX loss Cook example: USD depreciates against EUR by 0.75% → USD-denominated returns lose 0.75% when converted to EUR → −0.75%
Watch the direction carefully. "USD depreciates against EUR" = your USD bonds are worth less in EUR terms = negative FX contribution. Don't flip the sign.

Rolldown strategy — why it works
Buy further out on an upward-sloping curve. As time passes the bond rolls toward a lower-yield, higher-price point. You earn rolldown return on top of yield — as long as the curve stays stable and upward-sloping. This is why the 2-year zero-coupon (bought at 96.1169, expected to be worth 99.0090 in a year) earns more than the 1-year zero-coupon in the Thai bond example.
BPV / DV01 — same thing, different names
Dollar change in portfolio value for a 1bp (0.01%) move in rates.
BPV = Modified duration × Market value × 0.0001 Example: Duration 5 · MV $10M → BPV = 5 × $10M × 0.0001 = $5,000 per bp
Why it matters: Duration is a percentage — you can't subtract durations across portfolios of different sizes. BPV puts everything in the same unit (dollars per bp) so arithmetic works. Hedging: Nf = (BPV_target − BPV_portfolio) / BPV_futures Immunization: match asset BPV to liability BPV so a 1bp move hits both equally
Negative convexity — callable bonds
At high yields: call is out of the money, callable bond behaves like option-free bond — normal positive convexity. At low yields: call is deep in the money. Issuer's right to call becomes very valuable. Market knows bond may be called → price stops rising at normal pace → price-yield curve bends the wrong way → negative convexity. The asymmetry: less upside when rates fall (call caps price) but full downside when rates rise (call not exercised). You keep all the risk, lose some of the reward.
Why callable bonds must offer a higher yield: compensation for this asymmetric payoff. You are short the call option → you must be paid for it.
Forward rate bias
What theory says (UIP): high-yield currency should depreciate by exactly the yield differential — no free lunch. What actually happens: high-yield currencies depreciate by less than predicted, or not at all. The yield advantage is not fully offset. Implication: go unhedged into high-yielding currencies to earn excess returns. Amplify by borrowing in low-yield currencies (carry trade).
Carry trade = borrow in low-yield currency (JPY at 0.5%) · invest in high-yield currency (BRL at 10%) · pocket the spread. Works because forward rate bias means the low-yield currency doesn't appreciate enough to wipe out the carry.
Stable yield curve — what generates excess return
When rates don't move, you cannot earn excess return from predicting rate direction. The only way to beat the benchmark is to earn more carry.
StrategyWorks under stable curve?Why
Increase duration above benchmarkYesLonger bonds earn more yield + rolldown without rate rise penalty
Add leverageYesBorrow at short rates, invest in longer bonds, pocket the spread
Reduce duration below benchmarkNo — least likelyShorter bonds earn less yield, no rate protection needed, guaranteed underperformance vs benchmark
Reducing duration only makes sense if you expect rates to RISE — then shorter portfolio loses less than benchmark. Under a stable curve it just sacrifices yield for nothing.
